How Do I Choose K in K-Means Clustering?

Compute WCSS for several values of K, plot it, and pick the K where the curve bends and then flattens. If no clear bend appears, compare the cluster sizes against the problem itself, check that assignments stay stable across random restarts, and use a silhouette score as a second opinion.

What WCSS Measures

WCSS is the within-cluster sum of squares. For every point, take its squared distance to the centroid of the cluster it belongs to, then add those values over all points:

WCSS = sum over clusters k of sum over points x in k of ||x - mu_k||^2

A lower WCSS means tighter clusters. That does not make it a score to minimise: WCSS always falls as K grows, because more centroids can only shorten the distances. When K equals the number of points, WCSS is exactly 0 and the clustering says nothing.

Read the Elbow

Between K=2 and K=3 the WCSS falls by 185.21. Between K=3 and K=4 it falls by only 1.01. The slope changes sharply at K=3, so K=3 is the elbow and the value to pick. The three groups in the data are exactly what the cut at K=3 recovers.

Read the drop column, not the WCSS column. A small WCSS at K=6 (1.12) is lower than the WCSS at K=3 (3.02), but K=6 spends three extra centroids to remove 1.90 units of error. That is the trade the elbow method makes visible.

When the Elbow Is Unclear

  • Silhouette score. For each point, compare its mean distance to its own cluster (a) with its mean distance to the nearest other cluster (b). The score is (b - a) / max(a, b) and runs from -1 to 1. Average it per K and take the K with the highest mean; this does not depend on the WCSS scale.
  • Stability across restarts. Run k-means with different seeds and compare the final assignments. If K=4 gives a different partition on every seed while K=3 repeats, K=3 is the better answer.
  • Domain limits. If the clusters will be acted on by a person, a K that produces one cluster of 2 points and one of 400 points is usually wrong even when its WCSS is low.
